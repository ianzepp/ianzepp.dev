The main story · Sept 2025—present

A one-person lab, run at industrial pace.

For the last ten months I have done research and development full time with AI agents: a programming language and its compiler, two agent runtimes, a multi-tenant platform, MCP security tooling, and the evaluation harnesses that keep all of it honest.

The claim worth examining is not the volume—it is the method. I have been working out a repeatable way to direct, constrain, test, and audit AI-assisted engineering: goals and invariants written down before code, work split into bounded phases, commits used as checkpoints, and completion audited separately from implementation.

The operating method

How the factory runs.

Reconstructed from 9,588 archived session transcripts, this is the loop on most days, across most systems. The agents supply throughput. The judgment stays human.

  1. Write the goal first.

    The intended end state and its invariants go into a document that outlives the conversation. So far: 216 goal documents, 43 campaign plans, 91 ledgers.

  2. Split into bounded phases.

    Parallel agents get non-overlapping scopes. Read-only scouts go ahead of architecture changes; worktrees isolate anything that might collide.

  3. Implement one coherent slice.

    Format, lint, test, inspect the diff—in that order, every time. Weak tests and policy evasion get rejected, not merged.

  4. Commit as punctuation.

    A commit is a cognitive checkpoint between design and the next phase, not an end-of-day dump.

  5. Audit separately.

    Correctness, security, performance, and documentation each get their own pass—and goal completion is judged apart from implementation completion.

My job

Pick the problem and the invariant. Challenge the architecture and the naming. Decide clean-break versus compatibility. Partition the parallel work, test outcomes independently, and decide when the evidence is sufficient.

The agents’ job

Reconnaissance, implementation throughput, review passes, documentation, and the repetitive transformations nobody should do by hand.

In May 2026, one goal produced seven committed phases and separate acceptance proofs—and an audit that refused to call the goal complete while recovery requirements remained open. That refusal is the point.

The skill library

Make the model’s job easier.

Over the last six months, these repo-owned skills have grown into a cohesive layer of systems-engineering automation with one design goal: make the LLM’s job easier. Nearly every session on this page ran through them.

/fleet

The newest, and a favorite: Mind, Head, and Hand roles for multi-agent project loops, with stewardship and dead-man continuation. Built on Vivi mailspaces, so agents coordinate over mail instead of shared memory.

/campaign · /factory · /delivery

The work-flow spine, used on practically everything. Campaigns and sub-campaigns organize the flow of work; a factory defines the phases for each unit; every phase gets a ledger that tracks it to completion.

/polish

A per-file improvement loop backed by a deterministic analyzer that moves judgment calls out of the model and into repeatable checks. Polish is what keeps boilerplate LLM code from becoming slop.

/housekeeping

Repository hygiene with ratchets: not all code is perfect, so exceptions are explicit—and get ratcheted up or down per repo as the code earns it. This is how a codebase stays in clean, competent form.

/cassio

The skill behind the evidence on this page. Converts Claude, Codex, and OpenCode sessions into readable Markdown transcripts—and extracts training data for customized LLM tuning.

/mail + Vivi

Arbitrary local mailspaces for agents and projects. Vivi grew out of the mail skill and became the substrate Fleet coordinates over—private email treated as durable agent infrastructure.

The backstory

A career in four acts.

Four acts, dated from executed agreements, payroll records, and public archives where those exist. The evidence ledger below says so when they don’t.

1996—2009

Rifles, then a KDE front end for Nmap.

Four years in the Marine Corps—0351 Assaultman, out as a Corporal with an honorable discharge—left me with a bias toward responsibility that never wore off. In January 2000 I released KMap, a C++/Qt front end for Nmap. The Nmap 2.50 release notes mention it by name, which is still my favorite line of third-party evidence.

The path from there was not direct: five years selling real estate in Florida before joining Beatport’s API team in Denver in 2008, where electronic music distribution ran on Zend PHP, enterprise Java, Mule, Spring, Hibernate, Squid, Akamai, and MySQL.

USMC / 0351 / E-4C++ / Qt / KDEPHP / Java
2009—2017

Salesforce, scale, and the founder turn.

At Red Hat I worked on a multilingual APAC Partner Center built on Salesforce—Korea, Japan, and China, twenty-odd sandboxes. roundCorner hired me in 2011 as Chief Scientist; by the end I was VP Engineering, and the team had grown from four people to twelve, spread across four continents.

In 2014 I founded Minted.IO and spent 2015 self-funding a metadata-driven platform and API business. After a year inside Salesforce as a Technical Solution Architect, Motivis acquired the Minted.IO assets in February 2017. My VP offer was written into the acquisition.

Chief Scientist → VP EngTeam 4 → 12Founder / acquired
2017—2024

Platform leadership, then enough.

At Motivis I ran platform R&D—roughly ten engineers on a TypeScript, Node.js, PostgreSQL, and Google Cloud learning platform—then shifted to independent architecture and strategy consulting through Minted Geek, my Estonian company, from 2019.

By 2021 I stopped taking work. Early retirement was a genuine attempt to be finished with the industry, and for about four years, it held.

2025—NOW

The tools changed. I came back.

In August 2025 I pulled two decades of old source out of storage—KMap, the Motivis-era platform, payment systems—and started committing again. In October I barely touched a keyboard: two commits. Then November arrived, and with it the start of a 117-day unbroken streak.

What it became is the lab described above: a language and its compiler, two runtimes, a platform, security tooling, and thousands of recorded trials measuring whether any of it actually works. The receipts are in the ledger below.
